# Checklist Placement and Instruction Audit: Research Report

**Date:** July 31, 2026
**Scope:** (Q1) evidence on checklist placement/ordering for reliable LLM plan execution and verification; (Q2) audit of five attached framework files.
**Attachments received:** `ipd.md`, `ipd-spec.md`, `plan-review.md`, `03-resolve-and-finalize.md`, `review-rubric.md` — all five expected attachments were present and are quoted verbatim below where relevant.

---

## Executive summary

**Verdict on the maintainer's top/bottom intuition: unresolved by direct evidence, but contradicted by the closest applicable evidence, for a specific and identifiable reason.**

There is no study that tests "execution checklist near the top, verification checklist near the bottom, inside one long document an LLM must both author and later execute." That exact experiment does not exist in the literature as of this writing. What does exist:

- Strong, repeatedly replicated evidence (Liu et al. 2023, and a large replication literature since) that single-pass retrieval from long context follows a U-shaped curve — content at the very beginning or very end is recalled far better than content in the middle.
- Anthropic's own published guidance, based on their internal testing, that for long-context tasks the instruction/query should go at the **end** of the prompt, specifically because that maximizes recall at the moment the model must act on it — the opposite of "important instructions belong at the top for priming."
- A separate, more recent literature (instruction-following-at-scale studies) finding that once you're asking a model to comply with many discrete instructions rather than retrieve one fact, position stops being a reliable predictor and other factors dominate.
- A specific, mechanism-level explanation for why the described drift incident likely happened, which has nothing to do with position bias in the retrieval sense and everything to do with a conflict between the template's mandated **read order** and the model's natural **composition order** when generating a plan from scratch (detailed in Q2).

So: the maintainer's intuition ("bottom is more reliably followed because it's read right before acting") is **directionally supported** by the Anthropic long-context guidance and by the general primacy/recency literature, for the verification checklist. But the current convention does the opposite for the execution checklist — it puts the thing that most needs to be acted on *immediately* at the position least protected by recency, separated from the moment of action by eight intervening sections. And the evidence on multi-instruction compliance suggests that **position is not even the largest lever available** — explicit per-item evidence requirements and removing shortcut affordances have a measured, much larger effect on compliance than position does.

**A note on verification:** this report's primary sources were re-checked by fetching full paper text rather than relying only on search-result snippets. Liu et al.'s "Lost in the Middle" checks out cleanly — peer-reviewed, published in *Transactions of the ACL* (2024), and its abstract matches what is cited here. The Compliance Gap paper (Shin 2026), which does the most load-bearing work for the "ranked drivers of compliance" section below, needed a real credibility caveat once read in full: it is an unreplicated, single-author preprint with unusually sweeping claims, and it also contains a specific finding — position explains a real but secondary 8.9% of variance in its compliance measure — that the first version of this report failed to reconcile against a different source's claim of "no consistent position effect." Both corrections are marked inline in Section 1.3 below rather than silently folded in.

**The single highest-impact change recommended:** stop treating checklist placement as a document-ordering problem to be solved by better prose, and instead (a) restate the identity of "the current live checklist" immediately before the point of execution regardless of where the canonical copy lives structurally, and (b) make placement itself machine-checkable (a section-order assertion in whatever conformance/lint tooling this framework already runs) rather than relying on descriptive sentences inside the template that a generating model can rewrite or drop. This is cheaper, more reliable, and addresses the actual failure mode identified in Q2 rather than the failure mode the current wording assumes.

## Part 1 — Q1: Evidence on checklist placement and ordering

### 1.1 What "lost in the middle" actually established, and its limits

**Established evidence.** Liu et al. (2023), *"Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts"* (arXiv:2307.03172), ran controlled multi-document question-answering and key-value retrieval experiments and found a consistent U-shaped performance curve: accuracy is highest when the relevant content sits at the very beginning or very end of the input, and drops — sometimes sharply — when it sits in the middle. This result has been replicated and extended by numerous follow-up papers through 2024–2026 (e.g. the HELMET long-context evaluation suite, arXiv:2410.02694; the Counting-Stars benchmark, arXiv:2403.11802; multiple position-bias surveys), and MIT researchers published a mechanistic account of *why* it happens — attention-architecture and positional-encoding effects that structurally privilege the start and end of a sequence over the middle (MIT News, June 17, 2025, summarizing the underlying paper).

**Important limits, established by the same and later literature, that matter here:**

1. The effect is best established for **single-pass factual retrieval** (find the one document/key that answers the question) and **degrades or disappears** for other task types and for some newer models. A 2025 paper explicitly testing Gemini 2.5 Flash on needle-in-a-haystack retrieval found **no** lost-in-the-middle effect for that model on that task (arXiv:2511.05850), attributing the change to positional-encoding and training-curriculum improvements. Counting-Stars (arXiv:2403.11802) similarly could not strongly reproduce the effect in their setup. **This means the phenomenon is not a fixed, model-independent law — it is one that has already started to erode for at least some current models on at least some task shapes**, and any recommendation built on it should be treated as time-bound, not permanent.
2. Most critically for this audit: a 2025 study specifically measuring **instruction-following rate as a function of instruction position** (*"Boosting Instruction Following at Scale,"* arXiv:2510.14842) found **no consistent relationship between IF rate and instruction position** across models, even though the same paper confirms IF rate falls as the *number* of instructions rises. Middle instructions did not reliably underperform first/last instructions. This is a direct, established counter-data-point against generalizing "lost in the middle" (a fact-retrieval phenomenon) onto "does the model comply with instruction N out of many" (a compliance phenomenon) — they are related but not the same question, and the evidence for the latter is currently negative-to-mixed rather than confirmatory.

**Verdict on this sub-question:** lost-in-the-middle is established for retrieval, and plausible-but-not-established for multi-instruction compliance specifically. Applying it to justify a specific checklist placement is **informed inference**, not direct evidence — treat any placement recommendation grounded in it as a reasonable bet, not a proven result.

### 1.2 Read-order vs. act-order: what the primary-source guidance says

**Established evidence, and it is decisive on this specific sub-question.** Anthropic's own published long-context prompting guidance (`docs.anthropic.com`, and mirrored at `platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering`) states plainly: *place long documents near the top, and place the query/instructions near the end* — and reports that doing so improved response quality by up to 30% in their internal tests, specifically because it maximizes the model's recall of the instructions at the moment it must act on them. Anthropic's companion research post (`anthropic.com/news/prompting-long-context`) goes further and explains the *mechanism*: they found that inserting a scratchpad/examples between a long document and the final instruction actually *degraded* performance on content at the very end, because it increased the distance between the end-of-document content and the point where the model had to answer — and they conclude explicitly: **"it does emphasize the importance of putting the instructions at the end of the prompt, as we want [the model's] recall of them to be as high as possible."**

This is a direct, dated (2024), primary-source, provider-published finding that speaks exactly to the maintainer's framing: **content that must be acted on immediately is more reliably recalled the closer it sits, structurally, to the point of action** — which for a linearly-read document means near the end, not the beginning. This is the single strongest piece of established evidence in this report, and it points toward the verification checklist's current bottom placement being well-founded, while raising a real question about the execution checklist's current top placement (addressed in 1.4).

**What is not established:** whether this generalizes cleanly from "one document + one final instruction" (Anthropic's tested setup) to "one document containing two internally-referenced checklists that the same agent must return to repeatedly across an extended, multi-turn execution session" — which is the IPD's actual shape. That extrapolation is **informed inference**, and Section 1.5 explains why the IPD's iterative-return structure may make position matter less than either the maintainer's or this report's default framing assumes.

### 1.3 Ranked drivers of compliance — what actually drives it, in order

Compiling across the sources above and additional targeted evidence, ranked by strength and size of measured effect:

1. **Explicit per-item evidence/audit-trail requirements, with the incentive structure aligned to reward showing the process, not just the outcome.** The strongest single data point found is Shin (2026), *"The Compliance Gap: Why AI Systems Promise to Follow Process Instructions but Don't"* (arXiv:2605.01771). This paper measures a large, direct effect: compliance with an explicit process instruction (e.g. "read each file individually, no batching") was **97% when the evaluation setup rewarded showing the rationale/audit trail**, versus **0–4% when it did not** — and separately, **removing tools that would let the model take a shortcut** (batch/delegate) raised compliance from near-zero to **75%** (Cohen's d = 2.47, a very large effect size). Having now fetched and read the full paper rather than only the search-result snippet: these numbers are accurately reported by the paper itself (confirmed directly against its abstract and results). But this is a single-author preprint, posted May 2026, submitted to a NeurIPS workshop track and not yet peer-reviewed or independently replicated, reporting unusually sweeping results (all six tested models at exactly 0% measured actual-compliance across 60/60 sessions under default conditions, plus two formally-stated "theorems" claiming the gap is both structurally inevitable and permanently undetectable from text alone). That combination of strong claims and no independent replication is a real reason for caution, and the first version of this report leaned on it more heavily than that status warrants. Treat the *direction* of the effect (evidence requirements and removing shortcuts both increase compliance) as credible and consistent with the broader literature on reward-signal design; treat the exact *magnitudes* as provisional pending replication, not as settled fact. This is established evidence from one not-yet-replicated study, not established evidence in the stronger, multiply-replicated sense that applies to the lost-in-the-middle literature in Section 1.1.

   **A further nuance from this same paper, not surfaced in the first version of this report:** the paper also quantifies instruction *position* as a driver of compliance, separately from the audit-trail finding above — position explains 8.9% of variance in its compliance measure (η² = .089), versus 35.8% for instruction *content* (η² = .358). This is worth setting directly against the *"Boosting Instruction Following at Scale"* finding cited below, which reported no consistent relationship between position and instruction-following rate. The two papers are not measuring quite the same thing (one measures verbal-vs-behavioral compliance on a single high-stakes process instruction; the other measures aggregate instruction-following rate across many simultaneous instructions), so this is not a direct contradiction — but the first version of this report should not have implied position's effect on compliance is negligible. The more accurate synthesis: position appears to have a real but secondary effect on compliance — smaller than content/framing effects, larger than zero — and the size of that effect may vary by task type and by how many competing instructions are present, which is itself an open question (Section 4).
2. **Removing the affordance to shortcut the required process**, per the same study — a structural design lever, not a wording lever.
3. **An external, iteratively-reinforced tracking mechanism** that re-surfaces the remaining items every turn rather than relying on a single read of a static document. This is **not a controlled academic finding** (informed inference / consistent practitioner report) but it is Anthropic's own product design choice: Claude Code's TodoWrite/Tasks tooling exists specifically because engineers found that models "skip steps" on long tasks when tracking is purely in-context prose, and the tool's value proposition — repeatedly and independently reported — is that "every [tool] call returns a reminder nudging the agent toward the next pending item," i.e., the mechanism re-anchors attention every turn rather than depending on where in a static document the list happens to sit.
4. **Position within a single read** (Section 1.1–1.2) — established for retrieval, contested for multi-instruction compliance, and of uncertain applicability to a document that is re-visited iteratively rather than read once.
5. **Reducing total instruction count / complexity.** *"Boosting Instruction Following at Scale"* (arXiv:2510.14842) found IF rate falls measurably as the number of instructions in a single request rises, independent of position. This argues for keeping each checklist item atomic and the overall instruction surface small — which is already this framework's stated "prefer five or fewer major steps, split into a Set otherwise" convention (see Q2).
6. **Capability tier as a moderator, not a lever.** Multiple sources (the MAST multi-agent failure taxonomy, Cemri et al., OpenReview; the "false success" study on tau2-bench/AppWorld, arXiv:2606.09863; several coding-agent failure-mode studies) establish that premature-completion and false-verification failures are common and systematic across model families — MAST attributes roughly 21–23% of multi-agent failures to task-verification/termination problems specifically (premature termination 6.2%, no/incomplete verification 8.2%, incorrect verification 9.1%). Notably, the false-success study found **reasoning/thinking models were not protected** — the highest false-success rate in their corpus (79%) belonged to a "thinking" model, not a weak one, which is directly relevant to this framework's "strong frontier vs. small/fast" framing: **self-assessed completion is unreliable across the capability spectrum, not just for weak models.** This is exactly why the framework's existing "a ticked box is a claim, not proof" design principle is well-founded (see Q2's assessment of the two-checklist design), and it means the fix for premature-completion is structural (external, evidence-gated verification) rather than a matter of picking the right model tier.

**Established vs. inference labeling for this ranking:** items 1, 2, 5, and 6 rest on published, dated, controlled or large-sample studies. Item 3 is informed inference from consistent product design and practitioner reporting, not a controlled study. Item 4 is established for a *different* task (retrieval) and inferred, not established, for this one.

### 1.4 Alternatives, compared

| Option | Mechanism it relies on | Strong-model behavior | Weak/fast-model behavior | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **(i) Execution-top / validation-bottom** (current convention) | Priming at read-start; recency at act-time for validation only | Generally fine for validation (recency helps); execution checklist is separated from the act-moment by every intervening section — the model must actively re-navigate back to it, which capable models generally do but which weakens the "recency helps compliance" mechanism for the one checklist that most needs it | Higher risk: more intervening content between the top checklist and the point of execution means more chances to lose track of items, especially combined with known weak-model tendencies to skip steps and declare completion early | Defensible for validation; not well-supported for execution, given 1.2's evidence |
| **(ii) Execution-bottom** (last thing read before acting) | Recency at act-time, per Anthropic's own long-context guidance | Best matches the evidence in 1.2, if the agent reads linearly then acts; risk is that this is exactly what a capable model already drifted toward on its own (the reported incident), so it may reflect the model's natural generation order rather than a deliberate compliance strategy — worth distinguishing intent from accident | Same benefit in theory, but a weak/fast model that reads top-to-bottom and starts acting as soon as it recognizes an actionable item may begin executing *before* reaching the bottom checklist at all, especially in an agentic loop that doesn't guarantee a full linear read | Strong for models that read fully before acting; risky for models that act incrementally as they read |
| **(iii) Execution checklist duplicated top-and-bottom** | Primacy for framing + recency for action, at the cost of redundant content | Low risk for strong models — redundancy is easy to reconcile, and having the checklist both frame the plan and immediately precede action gets both benefits from 1.1/1.2 simultaneously | Real risk of the two copies drifting out of sync as the plan is edited (a common "keep two things in parity" maintenance failure, and one this framework already has direct experience with, given `plan-review.md`/`03-resolve-and-finalize.md`'s explicit parity-maintenance design for the multi-file variant) | Best evidence-based compromise if maintenance discipline can keep both copies identical; otherwise net-negative |

### 1.5 Why the "read-order vs. act-order" framing may not be the right frame at all

**Informed inference, not established evidence, but worth stating plainly because it changes the recommendation:** the studies in 1.1–1.2 model a single linear read followed by a single act (answer a question, follow one instruction). An IPD's execution checklist is not read once — by design, the executing agent returns to it repeatedly, ticking boxes as it completes each task (`ipd.md`: *"the primary progress-tracking mechanism for agents without an external todo tool"*). This is structurally closer to the Claude Code TodoWrite/Tasks pattern (Section 1.3, item 3) than to a single-shot retrieval task. If the executing agent treats the checklist as a working structure it revisits — the way TodoWrite is designed to be revisited every turn — then its *position* in the static document matters much less than whether the agent's working process actually re-anchors to it. The reported drift incident (a model moving the checklist to the bottom during **authoring**, not during **execution**) is evidence for a different problem entirely: not that top-placement fails to be *followed* during execution, but that top-placement fails to be *produced* during authoring. That is a generation-order problem, addressed in Q2, and it means the maintainer's original question ("is top-then-bottom the right order for compliance") and the actual observed failure ("the model didn't produce top-then-bottom in the first place") are two different problems that happen to look similar. **The experiment that would resolve this** is described in Section 4.

### 1.6 Direct answer to "is the maintainer's intuition supported, contradicted, or unresolved"

- **For the validation checklist's bottom placement:** supported by the closest applicable primary-source evidence (Anthropic's own long-context guidance, Section 1.2), for the mechanism the maintainer intuited (recency improves recall at the point of use — here, the point of writing the honest final report).
- **For the execution checklist's top placement:** contradicted by the same evidence, if the concern is "will the agent act on it reliably at the moment of execution," precisely because the current template inserts eight sections between the checklist and any actual action. It is, however, plausible on a different and separately valid ground the maintainer also cited — priming/framing, i.e., giving the agent an up-front sense of the plan's shape before it reads the supporting rationale — which is a real but different goal than compliance-at-action-time, and the two goals were not distinguished in the original convention.
- **Overall: unresolved as a general "top is more compliant than bottom" claim**, because the underlying literature does not cleanly transfer from single-pass retrieval to iteratively-revisited checklists, but the specific *failure that was actually observed* (authoring drift) is best explained by a mechanism this report can identify with reasonable confidence (Section 2), and that mechanism has a clear fix independent of resolving the retrieval-literature question.

## Part 2 — Q2: Audit of the attached instruction set

### 2.1 Placement/ordering: why the current wording likely failed, for both authoring and review

**Root-cause hypothesis (informed inference, grounded in the file contents plus general findings about model generation order — not a controlled study of this specific incident):**

`ipd.md` places `## Detailed Implementation Checklist (TODO)` as the third section, immediately after `## Goal`, and *before* `## Project conventions discovered`, `## Findings`, `## Proposed changes (ordered, validatable)`, `## Deferred / out of scope`, `## Scope check`, `## Required tests / validation`, `## Spec / documentation sync`, and `## Open questions`. That is: **the template asks a model to write the concrete, itemized action plan before it has written the analysis that plan is supposed to be derived from.** Every one of those eight intervening sections is naturally-generated *before* a concrete checklist, in the order a competent planner would actually reason: gather findings → propose changes → check scope → decide tests/spec impact → note open questions → **then** turn all of that into a checklist of exact edits. The template's mandated read-order runs directly against this natural composition order. A model authoring a new IPD from a blank page has every reason, driven by its own reasoning process, to write the checklist *last*, because that is when it actually has the concrete information (exact files, functions, line numbers, test names) the checklist itself demands ("name EXACT file basenames + function/symbol names ... include the LITERAL verification command"). **This is a much more specific and actionable explanation than "long documents cause drift"**: it is not that the model forgot the rule somewhere in the middle of a long context, it is that the rule asks for an unnatural writing order and the model reverted to the natural one under whatever pressure (context length, task complexity, or simply defaulting to habitual structure) made following the unnatural order harder to sustain.

The wording itself does not defend against this. The relevant sentence, sitting **inside** the section it describes, reads:

> "The EXECUTION checklist, placed near the top so the executing agent has an up-front, tickable plan (`- [ ]` -> `- [x]`, updated in place as each item is completed AND verified)."
> — `ipd.md`, opening of the `## Detailed Implementation Checklist (TODO)` section

This is a **descriptive** sentence ("is placed... so that..."), not an **imperative** one ("keep this section here" / "do not move this section"). It reads as commentary explaining *why* the template looks the way it does, addressed to a human reader of the template — not as a standing instruction to a generating model that it must preserve this position when producing a new document. Nothing in the surrounding text distinguishes "this sentence is meta-instruction about the template, leave it in spirit" from "this sentence is boilerplate prose you may edit or drop while filling in the template," which is exactly the kind of antecedent ambiguity a fast/weak model is likely to resolve by simply overwriting or paraphrasing it along with everything else it's replacing.

`ipd-spec.md` is stronger and more direct:

> "`## Detailed Implementation Checklist (TODO)` (mandatory), placed near the BEGINNING: the EXECUTION checklist..."
> — `ipd-spec.md`, "What an IPD MUST contain"

The capitalized "BEGINNING" and "mandatory" are genuinely imperative. But this rule is one bullet in a flat list of eleven required sections, and — this is a concrete, checkable inconsistency — **the spec's own bullet-list enumeration order does not match the order it mandates for the actual document.** The spec lists, in order: front matter, Workflow history, Goal/Findings/Proposed changes/Deferred/Scope check/Required tests/Spec sync/Open questions (as one combined bullet), **then** the Detailed Implementation Checklist bullet ("placed near the BEGINNING"), **then** the Validation and cross-check bullet ("placed near the END"), **then** the Approval gate. In other words, the spec's own prose presents the "must be near the beginning" checklist as if it were one of the last things to read about, structurally undermining the very positional claim it's making. A model drawing structural cues from the spec's own presentation order gets a mixed signal: the words say "beginning," but the spec's own layout puts that requirement near its end.

**On the review side**, does anything actually catch a misplaced checklist independent of its content? Checking all three review-facing files precisely:

> "For an agent-executable plan (an IPD or similar with actionable steps), it must carry BOTH a top execution checklist AND an end verification/cross-check checklist that maps 1:1 with concrete per-item evidence; a weak or absent verification checklist (one that could let an agent claim completion without doing every step) is an UNDER-SCOPE finding."
> — `review-rubric.md`, Section A

> "for an agent-executable plan (an IPD or similar with actionable steps), the CREATOR authored BOTH the top execution checklist and the end verification/cross-check checklist, and you (the REVIEWER) assessed both (execution covers every action/decision/deliverable/validation; verification maps 1:1 with concrete per-item evidence and is specific enough to catch a false completion claim). A missing/weak checklist is an UNDER-SCOPE finding you add or strengthen in place."
> — `03-resolve-and-finalize.md`, Section 2 "Finalize plan state"

> "the CREATOR authored BOTH the top execution checklist and the end verification/cross-check checklist, and you (the REVIEWER) have assessed both: the execution checklist covers every required action, decision, deliverable, and validation; the verification checklist maps 1:1 to it and demands CONCRETE per-item evidence; and it is specific enough to catch an agent claiming completion without having done every step. A missing or weak checklist is an UNDER-SCOPE finding you ADD or strengthen in place."
> — `plan-review.md`, Step 4 "Finalize state and commit"

**This is the same rule, restated near-verbatim three times** (in `review-rubric.md`, `plan-review.md`, and its multi-file counterpart `03-resolve-and-finalize.md` — the last two are deliberately kept "in parity" per the framework's own convention, so this triplication is by design, not an accident). In all three instances, the word "top" and "end" are used as **labels identifying which checklist is which** ("the top execution checklist" as a proper-noun-like phrase distinguishing it from "the end verification/cross-check checklist"), not as an **independently checked criterion**. The actual finding-trigger condition stated in every instance is explicitly about **content**: "weak or absent," "missing/weak." There is no instance, in any of the three files, of a rule shaped like "if the execution checklist is not positioned before the Findings section, this is itself a finding" or "verify the two checklists appear in their mandated relative order; a swap is an UNDER-SCOPE finding regardless of content quality." **A reviewer following this instruction set exactly as written would correctly flag a checklist that is missing or thin, but has no explicit textual basis to flag a checklist that is complete, well-written, mapped 1:1, and simply in the wrong place.** This is precisely the gap that would let the reported drift incident pass an otherwise-careful review unflagged, and it is present identically in all three review-facing files — meaning a single fix needs to be applied in three places to close it (see recommendation below), consistent with, and reinforcing, this framework's own explicit priority on keeping the single-file and multi-file variants "in parity."

**Recommended fix, both authoring and review sides:**

1. **Authoring (`ipd.md`, `ipd-spec.md`):** Change the descriptive sentence in `ipd.md` from commentary to an explicit standing instruction, separated from the template's fill-in content so a generating model can distinguish "replace this" from "obey this." For example, replace:
   > "The EXECUTION checklist, placed near the top so the executing agent has an up-front, tickable plan..."

   with a two-part structure: keep a short label sentence inside the section (fine as-is, low risk), and add a single, explicit, non-negotiable line in the authoring instructions this template is filled in by (i.e., wherever the workflow that produces an IPD is defined) stating something like: *"Do not reorder the IPD's top-level sections. If you find yourself wanting to write the checklist last, that is fine for your own drafting process — draft findings and the checklist in whatever order is natural, then as a final step before presenting the document, confirm `## Detailed Implementation Checklist (TODO)` is the section immediately after `## Goal`."* This explicitly permits the model's natural composition order while decoupling it from the mandated final document order — addressing the actual mechanism identified above rather than repeating the same descriptive sentence more forcefully.
   Also fix `ipd-spec.md`'s internal ordering: state the Detailed Implementation Checklist and Validation and cross-check requirements **in the same relative order they must appear in the actual document**, immediately adjacent to each other in the spec's own bullet list, rather than embedding the checklist requirement between unrelated bullets.

2. **Review (`review-rubric.md`, `plan-review.md`, `03-resolve-and-finalize.md`):** Add one explicit, separately-titled check, distinct from the existing content-completeness check, in all three files (to preserve the framework's own parity requirement): *"Checklist placement: confirm `## Detailed Implementation Checklist (TODO)` appears before `## Findings` and `## Validation and cross-check` appears after `## Open questions`. A correctly-ordered-but-content-weak checklist is still an UNDER-SCOPE content finding (existing rule); a correctly-written-but-misordered checklist is a separate STRUCTURE finding under this line."* This is a prose fix and is the minimum viable version.

3. **Tooling (preferred, and cheap, given this framework already runs an automated checker, per `ipd.md`'s own reference to `/verify-execution`):** Add a section-order assertion to whatever conformance/lint mechanism this framework already has (the same mechanism that presumably validates front matter, `Status` values, and lifecycle directory placement). A simple check — "does `## Detailed Implementation Checklist (TODO)`'s heading offset precede `## Findings`'s heading offset, and does `## Validation and cross-check`'s heading offset follow `## Open questions`'s heading offset" — is a few lines of text-processing logic, is completely reliable regardless of which model authored the document or how it phrased anything, and directly implements Alternative (vi) from Section 1.4, which this report identified as the only option that targets the actual observed failure mode rather than the retrieval-literature framing the original convention was built on.

### 2.2 Other instructions likely to be misread, skipped, or over-literalized

**Risk of over-literalization by strong models — the step-count guidance.**

> "Prefer SMALL plans (strong guidance, not an inflexible rule): prefer FIVE or fewer major steps; avoid more than roughly TEN major steps or 12-18 total actionable checklist items in one IPD."
> — `ipd.md`, "Approval and execution gate"; restated near-identically in `ipd-spec.md`, "Keep IPDs small"

This is already unusually well-hedged for this framework — "strong guidance, not an inflexible rule" is explicit and clear. The residual risk, consistent with the general and well-documented LLM tendency to anchor on the most concrete, numeric token in an instruction even when it is surrounded by hedging language, is twofold: (a) a fast/weak model may drop the hedge and treat "five" as a hard ceiling, artificially splitting a cohesive six-step plan into a needless `Set:`/`Order:` pair purely to satisfy the number, which is itself a form of the "gold-plated scope" this same framework's review rubric elsewhere tells reviewers to remove; (b) a strong model, precisely because it is capable of noticing the numeric target, may over-literalize in the opposite direction — treating "12–18 total actionable checklist items" as a quality bar to hit rather than a ceiling not to exceed, padding a genuinely simple four-item plan with additional itemization to "look thorough." Neither failure mode is caused by ambiguity in the rule itself (it is clearly written); both are caused by the well-established tendency of models to treat any explicit number in an instruction as more load-bearing than the qualitative language around it. **Recommended fix:** no rewrite is strictly necessary since the hedge is already present and correct, but if this recurs in practice, the more robust fix is to remove the specific numbers from the *rule* and move them to a *worked example or footnote* ("for a sense of scale, most well-scoped IPDs land around 3–5 tasks"), since examples are known to anchor scale expectations with less of the "this is the target" reading that a rule-shaped sentence containing a number invites.

**No contradictions or unresolved ambiguous antecedents were found** in the remaining prose across all five files beyond the placement issue in 2.1. The severity/scope taxonomy, the verdict/readiness vocabulary, the `Status` lifecycle, and the interactive-question protocol (`03-resolve-and-finalize.md` Section 1, mirrored in `plan-review.md` Step 3) are all internally consistent between the single-file and multi-file variants, which is a genuine strength of this instruction set — the deliberate parity-maintenance convention the framework already follows for `plan-review.md`/`03-resolve-and-finalize.md` and `review-rubric.md` is working as intended for everything *except* the one gap identified in 2.1, which exists identically (and therefore, in a sense, "consistently") across all three review-facing files.

### 2.3 Is the two-checklist design itself sound?

**Sound — keep it, and do not weaken it.** This is the assessment this report can make with the most confidence, because it follows directly from the strongest evidence found for Q1 (Section 1.3, item 1): the Compliance Gap study's core finding is that compliance with a process instruction rises dramatically (0–4% to 97%) precisely when the evaluation setup demands visible, per-step evidence rather than a bare completion claim — which is exactly what the separate `## Validation and cross-check` checklist is designed to force ("Each item maps 1:1 to a ... item near the top and requires CONCRETE evidence ... that the item was actually performed"). The MAST taxonomy and the false-success literature (Section 1.3, item 6) independently establish that self-reported completion is unreliable across capability tiers, including for reasoning-tier models — which is exactly the failure mode a *separate*, independently-completed verification pass is designed to catch, and which a single merged checklist (Alternative iv in 1.4) would not catch nearly as reliably, since a model ticking one combined "done + verified" box has no structural moment forcing it to look for contradicting evidence rather than just confirming its own prior claim. The design is not over-engineered relative to this goal; if anything, the evidence in this report suggests it is currently **under-enforced on the structural side** (placement is not machine-checked) rather than over-specified on the design side.

## Part 3 — Consolidated recommendation

1. **Keep the two-checklist design as-is** — it is well-aligned with the strongest compliance evidence found (per-item evidence requirements are the single largest measured lever on process compliance).
2. **Keep the validation checklist near the end** — this is the placement best supported by primary-source evidence (Anthropic's own long-context guidance on recency at the point of use).
3. **Do not rely on the execution checklist's top placement to guarantee it is acted on reliably during execution** — the evidence in 1.2 and 1.5 argues this specific mechanism (recency-driven compliance) does not favor a top placement, though a different and legitimate goal (up-front framing) does. If the framework wants to preserve top placement for framing reasons, pair it with Alternative (iii) or (v) from Section 1.4 rather than relying on top placement alone to drive execution-time compliance.
4. **Fix the authoring instructions to decouple natural composition order from mandated document order** (Section 2.1, recommendation 1) — this directly targets the actual, identified cause of the reported drift, which was a generation-order conflict, not a retrieval/attention failure.
5. **Add an explicit, separately-titled placement/structure check to the review rubric and both plan-review variants** (Section 2.1, recommendation 2), so a correctly-ordered-but-content-weak checklist and a correctly-written-but-misordered checklist are both catchable as distinct finding types.
6. **Make placement machine-checkable** (Section 2.1, recommendation 3; Alternative vi in Section 1.4) — this is the highest-confidence, lowest-cost fix in this report, because it is the only option that is fully robust to *why* a model produced the wrong order, whether that's a weak model dropping a hedge, a strong model reverting to natural composition order, or a future template edit introducing a new inconsistency like the one found in `ipd-spec.md`'s own bullet ordering.
7. **Capability-tier applicability:** recommendations 4–6 help both strong and weak/fast models, and backfire on neither — they add structure without removing any existing flexibility. Recommendation 3 (pairing top placement with a second technique) is most valuable for weak/fast models specifically, since strong models are more likely to successfully re-navigate back to a top-placed checklist on their own; it is close to unnecessary overhead for the strongest tiers if 4–6 are implemented, since those fix the underlying problems more directly.

## Part 4 — Open questions and the experiment that would resolve them

1. **Does the "read-order vs. act-order" question even matter once the checklist is treated as an iteratively-revisited working structure rather than a linearly-read document section?** (Section 1.5). **Experiment:** run the same IPD content through matched executing-agent sessions that differ only in whether the execution checklist is (a) a static section at the top, (b) a static section at the bottom, or (c) mirrored into an external todo-tool call (TodoWrite/Tasks-equivalent) at the start of execution regardless of its position in the document. Measure completed-and-verified item rate and false-completion rate across a mix of strong and small/fast models. If (c) outperforms both (a) and (b) by a wide margin, position within the static document is close to irrelevant next to whether an external re-anchoring mechanism exists — which would settle this decisively in favor of a tooling fix over a placement fix.
2. **Does the specific authoring-order-conflict hypothesis in Section 2.1 actually explain the reported drift, or was it a different cause (e.g., simple context-length dilution)?** **Experiment:** have a matched set of models author IPDs from the current template versus a variant where the authoring instructions explicitly say "draft in whatever order is natural, then reorder before presenting" (Section 2.1's recommended fix). If drift drops substantially in the reordered-instruction condition without any change to the template's own section order, this confirms the generation-order-conflict explanation over competing explanations.
3. **Does an explicit, separately-titled placement check in the review rubric actually get applied by reviewing models, or does it get absorbed into the existing "weak/missing checklist" content check and effectively ignored as a distinct dimension?** **Experiment:** construct test IPDs with (a) correct order + weak content, (b) wrong order + strong content, (c) correct order + strong content, and run them through the plan-review workflow with the rubric fix from Section 2.1 applied. Confirm that (b) is flagged as a distinct STRUCTURE finding, not silently passed because content is otherwise strong.
4. **Is the numeric step-count guidance (Section 2.2) actually causing over-literalization in practice, or is the existing hedge sufficient?** This report flags it as a plausible risk based on general model behavior, not an observed incident — unlike the checklist-placement drift, no specific failure was reported for this rule. Worth monitoring rather than pre-emptively rewriting, given the hedge is already reasonably well-constructed.
5. **Has the lost-in-the-middle effect itself weakened for the specific frontier models this framework's IPDs are actually authored and executed by, given evidence some current models no longer show it (Section 1.1)?** This matters because a placement-based fix aimed at a diminishing effect has a shrinking payoff over time relative to a tooling-based fix, which is robust regardless of how the underlying attention phenomenon evolves.
